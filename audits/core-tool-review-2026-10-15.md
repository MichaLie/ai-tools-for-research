**How evidence was weighed.** University procurement and deployment documentation establishes institutional provision, not how many researchers actually use a feature. Library guides and teaching exercises establish academic visibility and recommended workflows, not licenses or measured uptake. Publisher instructions provide particularly direct evidence of a publishing workflow. Vendor documentation establishes advertised capabilities, not community adoption or comparative quality. No representative, feature-level adoption survey was found in the sources reviewed, so these recommendations do not claim market-share rankings. General ChatGPT/Claude popularity, API-credit programs, GitHub stars, and vendor user counts were not treated as adoption of the specific product being reviewed.

**Office: one default, a meaningful alternative.** The case for Microsoft is its documented provision through university Office environments. The case for Claude is its cross-file context and a documented academic pilot. Neither proves superior scientific reasoning or reliable spreadsheet calculations. I treat coordinated preparation of documents, workbooks and slides as one Office-production use case here; I am not claiming feature parity between the implementations. If a future catalog entry isolates a demonstrated research workflow that only one can perform, it should be evaluated as that task rather than dismissed because both run in Office.

**Deep research: one delegated-report exemplar plus one exploration exemplar.** ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini overlap in the input–output job: a research question and selected sources become a cited investigation. Connected files do not by themselves establish an exclusive task: ChatGPT also documents supported connected sources, subject to account and workspace settings. [OpenAI documentation](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10500283-deep-research-in-chatgpt)

ChatGPT versus Gemini is the least decisive adoption comparison. Both have feature-specific institutional provision. I propose ChatGPT as the general catalog's reference exemplar, supported by the Illinois deployment, while acknowledging that the evidence cannot establish it as the globally better-adopted choice. This is an editorial tie-break. For a course whose students receive Gemini through their institution, swapping Gemini into that single slot would be equally defensible. Keeping both core would not add a distinct task.

STORM should stay only with its different job made explicit: helping the researcher discover perspectives and steer the developing understanding. Co-STORM's participant dialogue and evolving mind map support that distinction. Its open implementation also permits inspection and modification of the workflow; that alone is not a claim of reproducible outputs. The README's latest news is dated January 2025, so it should retain the existing maintenance caveat. No research job was run in this review, and the Lib4RI exercise is historical evidence. Local execution requires setup and model/retrieval resources; open-source code does not imply zero operating cost. [Project documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/stanford-oval/storm/main/README.md)

Applied task-first description, 34 words: “Explore a topic through perspective-guided questions and cited outlines. Co-STORM lets you steer an expert-agent discussion and inspect an evolving mind map; this MIT-licensed research prototype offers a web preview and customizable local setup.”

**Writing: distinguish translation, ongoing correction, and manuscript preparation.** DeepL's whole-document translation is a separate output. Grammarly earns its place through language correction embedded in the writer's ongoing communication. Paperpal earns its place through the manuscript workflow, including sources and submission checks. There is substantial feature overlap: Paperpal also corrects grammar and works in several writing environments. The reason to keep both is their different primary jobs and documented academic support, not their app surfaces or brand names.

Grammarly is therefore the weakest retained writing slot. Iowa State's non-renewal is a real signal that it can be redundant for some communities. Its continued provision elsewhere supports retaining the everyday language-correction exemplar for this broad catalog, but not presenting it as a necessary extra subscription. Michigan's configuration cannot be cited as adoption of Grammarly's generative features.

Jenni is a credible alternative, not a failed admission. Its academic recommendation and source-aware drafting are real. The overlap decision depends on current Paperpal capabilities: reference-library imports, drafting grounded in saved sources, citation insertion, and predictive continuation are now documented. These are capability claims rather than a head-to-head usability test. Paperpal's additional publisher-endorsed preflight workflow gives it the stronger reason to occupy the manuscript slot. This does not establish that its autocomplete or citation handling is better than Jenni's.

These are seven jobs represented by six tools; the purpose is not to force one tool per feature. The four approved moves retain useful alternative workflows in extended. None of the three general deep-research agents is treated as a replacement for systematic bibliographic searching or the catalog's specialist discovery tools. A submission preflight check is also not automatic journal reformatting: that gap remains.

Public evidence was reviewed on 15 October 2026. This review compares task coverage and documented academic provision, recommendation and publishing use. It does not measure actual usage, rank output quality, or certify feature-level reliability. The recommendation is strongest on removing duplicate tasks and less certain on choosing ChatGPT over Gemini and retaining Grammarly as a separate everyday-writing exemplar.
